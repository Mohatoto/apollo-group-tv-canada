For streaming TV, a mesh Wi-Fi system is usually the steadier choice for a whole home, and a Wi-Fi extender is a cheaper fix for one weak room. A basic extender repeats the signal over the same Wi-Fi it is trying to improve, so it can cut the speed that reaches the TV; mesh units are built to work together as one network. If the TV can be reached with a network cable, a cable beats both.

This guide compares the two on the points that matter for a TV that stays in one place: speed, steadiness, network names, placement and cost. It builds on our guides to [Wi-Fi or Ethernet for streaming](/guides/wifi-vs-ethernet/) and [router settings for streaming](/guides/router-settings-for-streaming/).

<h2 id="at-a-glance">Extender or mesh: which should you choose?</h2>

Choose a mesh system for a larger home, several floors or thick walls. Choose an extender for one weak room, a small space or a tight budget. Best Buy Canada calls mesh "a whole-home solution, ideal for larger homes" and an extender "a targeted solution".

| | Wi-Fi extender | Mesh Wi-Fi system |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | One device that repeats your router's signal | Several units (nodes) that form one network |
| Best for | One weak room, an apartment or condo, a tight budget | A larger home, several floors, thick walls |
| Speed at the TV | Can drop, because the repeating uses Wi-Fi; NETGEAR says a basic dual-band extender may reduce the available bandwidth by half | Held up better when the nodes have a dedicated link between them, such as a third band (How-To Geek) |
| Network name | Often a second name; you switch by hand | One name for the whole home |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Setup | Plug in, pair, choose a spot | Place and set up several units |

Best Buy Canada says mixing a mesh system and an extender "typically doesn't mesh (pun intended) well". Neither one adds speed that is not arriving from your internet provider. Best Buy Canada notes that mesh "isn't going to speed up the internet beyond what your plan allows".

<h2 id="need">How do you know a Wi-Fi extender or mesh is what you need?</h2>

You need one only if the Wi-Fi signal is weak where the TV sits. Run two speed tests before buying anything, one next to the router and one at the TV.

1. **Test next to the router** (on a phone held beside it, or on a laptop on a cable).
2. **Test at the TV** (on the TV itself, or on a phone held next to it).
3. **Compare both with what the stream needs.** The [FAQ](/faq/) on this site lists 10 Mbps or more for HD and 25 Mbps or more for 4K, and our guide on [internet speed for HD and 4K](/guides/internet-speed-for-streaming/) shows how to add up several screens.

How to read the result:

- **Slow next to the router too:** the problem is the connection from your provider. An extender or mesh system will not fix it, so contact your provider.
- **Fast next to the router, slow at the TV:** the signal is not reaching the TV. This is what an extender, a mesh system or a cable fixes.
- **Fast at both, but the picture still pauses:** the signal is not the cause. Try the [buffering checklist](/guides/fix-buffering/) and the router settings in our [router guide](/guides/router-settings-for-streaming/) first.

<h2 id="extender">How does a Wi-Fi extender work, and why can it slow the stream?</h2>

A Wi-Fi extender is one device that picks up your router's Wi-Fi and rebroadcasts it further into the home. NETGEAR and D-Link describe it as "one simple device that extends your Wi-Fi signal by rebroadcasting it further into your home". Most extenders receive the router's signal over Wi-Fi, so the same radio has to listen to the router and talk to your devices.

That is why a basic extender can slow the stream down. NETGEAR says a single-channel dual-band extender "may reduce the available bandwidth by half". Best Buy Canada puts it more gently: the rebroadcasting "can result in reduced speeds, though it will still be faster than without one". In a room with almost no signal, a slower connection is still better than none.

Three more things to know about extenders:

- **Separate network name.** NETGEAR says extenders "broadcast on a separate WiFi name", so devices must switch networks by hand. For a TV that never moves, you set this once. NETGEAR adds that switching can cause "streaming to buffer and reconnect" on devices that move around.
- **Some models take a cable.** NETGEAR and D-Link say certain models also allow Ethernet or Powerline connections to the router, which avoids the Wi-Fi hop.
- **One extender is the limit.** How-To Geek warns that chaining two extenders to reach a far room makes Wi-Fi "noticeably slower"; once more than one is needed, it calls a mesh system "often the better option".

<h2 id="mesh">How does a mesh system work?</h2>

A mesh system uses several units, called nodes, placed around the home that act as one network. NETGEAR says mesh "uses multiple devices around your home to create one seamless network". One node usually connects to your router by Ethernet cable, and the others spread the signal through the home. Each node has the same network name, so the TV never has to switch.

What keeps mesh steadier than a basic extender is how the nodes talk to each other, called the backhaul:

| Mesh type | How the nodes link | Effect on streaming |
|---|---|---|
| Tri-band | One of the system's three bands is used for the link between nodes, and the other two serve your devices (How-To Geek) | The link does not compete with the TV for airtime |
| Dual-band | The link shares bandwidth with your other devices (How-To Geek) | Performance is lower than tri-band |

Not every mesh system is the same, and a cheap dual-band mesh set may not be much better than a good extender. Check the box or the maker's page for the words "tri-band" or "dedicated backhaul".

<h2 id="roaming">Does a TV benefit from mesh roaming?</h2>

Not much. A big selling point of mesh is that phones and laptops move from node to node by themselves. D-Link says devices "will connect to the closest node automatically and without any disruptions as you move around your house", where an extender means "a disconnection while switching manually".

A TV does not move. This is our own reasoning, not a quote from a source: for a TV, the reasons to pick mesh are the dedicated link between nodes and the single network name, not roaming. If only the TV has a weak signal, a single extender, or a mesh node placed near the TV and wired to it, can solve it without replacing the whole network.

<h2 id="placement">Where should you put an extender or mesh unit?</h2>

Put the unit about halfway between the router and the weak area, where the signal is still good. Setting it in the weak room defeats the purpose, because it can only repeat a signal that it receives well. TP-Link's guidance for its range extenders gives these rules:

- **Halfway.** "Place the extender roughly halfway between your router and the area with a weak signal." If walls are in the way, move it closer to the router.
- **Height.** Keep it at about the same height as the router, around four feet off the ground, and not on the floor or in a corner.
- **Line of sight.** "A clear line of sight between the router and the extender is ideal."
- **Away from interference.** Keep it away from mirrors, large metal surfaces, microwaves, cordless phones, refrigerators and baby monitors.
- **If the wireless link is unstable.** TP-Link suggests an Ethernet cable from the router to the extender, with the extender in access point mode.

TP-Link's page is written for its own extenders (last updated 2 July 2021), but the same placement logic applies to mesh nodes. Mesh nodes can be moved easily; D-Link says they "can be relocated" and that coverage grows by adding nodes.

<h2 id="cable">Is a network cable better than either?</h2>

Yes, when you can run one. A cable does not depend on the signal and does not share airtime, so it is the most dependable link for a TV. See [Wi-Fi or Ethernet for streaming](/guides/wifi-vs-ethernet/) for the full comparison. Cables and extenders or mesh can also work together:

- **A cable to a mesh node near the TV.** One node connects to the router by cable, and a second node by the TV can give the TV a wired port.
- **A cable to an extender in access point mode.** TP-Link describes this as the fallback when a wireless link is not stable.
- **Powerline adapters.** They send the network through the home's electrical wiring; results depend on the wiring, so test the speed at the TV afterwards.

<h2 id="decide">How do you decide?</h2>

Follow the questions in order and stop at the first answer that applies:

1. **Are the speed tests slow next to the router?** Fix the connection with your provider first. Neither product helps.
2. **Can you run a cable to the TV?** Use the cable.
3. **Is only one room weak, in a small home or apartment?** Try one extender placed halfway, ideally with a cable to it if the model allows it.
4. **Is the home large, on several floors, or are several rooms weak?** Choose a mesh system, ideally tri-band or with a wired link between nodes.
5. **Do you already own an extender?** Test it by comparing the speed at the TV with and without it. Keep it only if the TV gets a clearly better result.
6. **After installing, test again at the TV.** Keep the change only if the speed and the picture improved.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is mesh better than a Wi-Fi extender for streaming?

For a whole home, usually yes, because mesh units form one network and many have a dedicated link between nodes. For one weak room, a single extender placed halfway between the router and the TV can be enough, and it costs less.

### Do Wi-Fi extenders cut your speed in half?

Basic ones can. NETGEAR says a single-channel dual-band extender may reduce the available bandwidth by half, because the extender uses the same Wi-Fi to listen to the router and to serve your devices. Models that connect to the router by cable avoid this.

### Where should I put a Wi-Fi extender for my TV?

About halfway between the router and the weak area, at roughly the same height as the router, with a clear line of sight and away from mirrors, metal and microwaves. Placing it in the weak room itself usually does not work.

### Can I use a mesh system and an extender together?

It is better not to. Best Buy Canada says mixing the two "typically doesn't mesh (pun intended) well". Pick one approach for the home.

### Will a mesh system or extender make my internet faster?

No. Neither can add speed beyond what your internet plan delivers; they only help the signal reach the TV. If the speed is low next to the router, contact your internet provider.

### Is a cable better than mesh for a TV?

Yes. A cable does not depend on the signal and does not share airtime, so it is the most dependable link. A mesh node near the TV connected by cable is a good way to get both.
